How much take-home salary do you keep
across countries?

Gross salary is just the starting number. After income tax and social security, your actual take-home varies enormously — Spain, Brazil, Israel, and the USA each keep a very different share of your paycheck.

The short answer

Take-home pay depends on three things: income tax brackets (progressive in most countries), social security contributions (mandatory, funding pensions and health), and personal credits and allowances specific to each country. On the same gross salary, effective deductions can range from ~10% in a low-tax country to 45%+ in a high-tax one.

For a rough comparison: a person earning $60,000 gross/year keeps roughly $42,000–$44,000 net in Spain, $46,000–$49,000 net in Brazil (single, standard INSS regime), and $43,000–$46,000 net in Israel — all illustrative, single-person figures. Why the gap is bigger than it looks

The numbers above show employee-side deductions only. In Brazil, employers pay an additional ~27–30% in social charges on top of gross salary — meaning the true cost to hire is far higher than the gross a worker sees. In Israel, pension contributions (at least 18.5% combined) are mandatory and partly employer-funded. In Spain, employer SS contributions add ~30% on top of gross. When comparing job offers across countries, total employer cost and net-in-pocket are both important.

What moves the needle most

  • Filing status: married couples with children get significant credits in Israel and the USA; Spain has family deductions; Brazil has dependents allowance.
  • Pension contributions: pre-tax pension contributions reduce your taxable income in most countries.
  • Income level: progressive brackets mean the deduction rate rises with income — the gap between gross and net widens at higher salaries.
  • Country of employment vs residence: tax treaties between countries can affect how income is taxed if you work remotely for a foreign employer.

Frequently asked questions

What is take-home pay and how does it differ from gross salary?
Take-home pay (net salary) is what lands in your bank account after income tax and social security are deducted from your gross salary. The gap between gross and net depends on each country's tax brackets, social security rates, personal allowances, and any applicable credits. It commonly ranges from 10% to 45%+ deducted depending on country and income level.
Which country taxes salaries the least in 2026?
Among major economies, the UAE (0% income tax), Singapore (~7–22% effective depending on income), and Switzerland have among the lowest combined burdens. In Latin America, Brazil's effective rate varies but typically lands 18–25% at middle incomes. Spain, France, and Germany run 30–45%+ at mid-to-high incomes. Israel sits in the 23–35% range depending on income. The ranking shifts significantly by income level — use the tool for your specific gross.
Does social security count toward take-home deductions?
Yes. Social security (called INSS in Brazil, Bituach Leumi in Israel, Seguridad Social in Spain, and similar names elsewhere) is a separate mandatory charge that reduces take-home pay on top of income tax. Employee-side social security rates range from near-zero in some countries to 15%+ in others. It funds pensions, healthcare, and unemployment insurance.
Does take-home salary comparison include relocation costs?
No — a take-home comparison shows what you keep after tax and social security in each country, but does not account for cost of living, housing, healthcare premiums, or relocation expenses. A $45,000 net in São Paulo, Madrid, and Tel Aviv has very different real purchasing power. Can I compare my take-home salary if I work remotely for a foreign company?
Yes, and the rules get complex. Remote workers are generally taxed where they are resident, not where the employer is based — but tax treaties between countries can override this.
